## Messaging Pillars

Everything on the site should trace back to one of these five. If a section doesn't serve one, cut it.

1. **One-time setup, built to last**: I come in and take care of what your system needs. Once it's in place, your team can maintain it themselves.
2. **Cost savings in the long run**: a well-built design system means fewer wasted credits every time AI touches your brand.
3. **Leverage the AI productivity boom, without losing quality**: let your team move at AI speed while your brand stays high-quality and consistent.
4. **Meet you where you are**: clean up and AI-ready the system you already have, or build a brand from scratch. Either way, I jump in and work with your team.
5. **Design governance that scales with you**: lets your team move fast while quality and consistency stay at the center of everything they ship.

---

## Education Themes (the "Learn" content)

Eight things someone needs to understand, roughly in the order a skeptical visitor would need them:

1. **What a design system is, and what makes one "agent-ready"**: not a Figma file, not a component library. A clear definition before anything else.
2. **Why it's so important right now, with AI tooling everywhere**: the urgency case, this mattered less before AI could produce at this volume.
3. **How it works alongside other AI tools**: how an agent-ready system plugs into the tools your team is already using, rather than replacing them.
4. **A rough idea of scope**: keep this light, but give a sense of how a cascading system gets built (foundation first, then layers on top).
5. **Easy to maintain once the foundation is set**: it evolves with the team; this isn't a one-and-done deliverable that goes stale.
6. **What you can build on top of it**: concrete examples of tools for marketing and product teams, not just design.
7. **Proof it works elsewhere**: Miro's Aura (70-80% support-ticket drop after going agent-ready), GitHub Primer's drift-scoring. Borrowed credibility while your own case studies are still young.
8. **The maturity gap**: only ~23% of design systems are structured well enough to benefit from AI tooling. This turns "well-structured design system" into the exception, not the norm, and frames the whole business as filling that gap.

**Structured as FAQ:**

**What is a design system, and what makes it "agent-ready"?**
> A design system is the shared set of rules, components, and language your team designs and builds from. An agent-ready one takes that a step further: it's structured so an AI can read it correctly, compose from it, and get flagged when it drifts off-spec. That's the difference between AI that speeds up your team and AI that quietly drifts your brand off course.

**Why does this matter right now?**
> This has always mattered, it just used to be optional. Now that AI can produce at real volume, a system without a strong foundation doesn't just create inconsistency, it multiplies it, fast. Getting this right is what lets your team actually benefit from AI instead of cleaning up after it.

**How does it work alongside the AI tools we already use?**
> An agent-ready system isn't another tool competing for your team's attention. It's the layer that makes the tools you already use (Claude, Figma, your own internal agents) actually understand your brand instead of guessing at it.

**What's the scope of a build like this?**
> Every system is built in layers, from the ground up: foundational tokens first, then components, then the rules that hold it all together. Nothing here needs to happen all at once, and nothing gets built you don't need yet.

**Is this a one-time deliverable, or does it evolve with us?**
> Once the foundation is in place, it's genuinely easy to maintain. This isn't a deliverable that goes stale the day it ships. It grows alongside your team and your brand.

**What can we build on top of it?**
> A solid foundation isn't just a design-team win. It's what lets marketing and product teams build their own tools on top of the same brand-accurate base, without every request routing back through design.

**Does this actually work?**
> Miro spent a year making their design system agent-ready and saw support questions drop 70-80%. GitHub's Primer team built drift-scoring that opens PRs automatically when components fall out of spec. This isn't theoretical, it's a maturing practice, and most teams haven't caught up yet.

**Isn't this already how most design systems work?**

## Standalone Page: Education — "How Design Systems Work" (live at /agentic-systems)

Not part of the main homepage flow, its own page, linked from the Learn hub for the visitor who wants the real detail, not just the FAQ version. Structured around six sections, built from the cascade concept. Live order: The cascade → Why two token layers? → The agent contract → What's inside it → How it evolves → What you can build.

**Page headline (draft):**
> Design systems, explained.

**Subhead:**
> A design system stored as code, with tokens, components and rules in one repo that people and agents both read. From it you can build skills that produce on-brand decks, landing pages, documents and more.

**Section 1 — The cascade**
Headline: *One system, built to hold together*
> A design system isn't a list of colors and button styles, it's an architecture. Every piece connects to the one above it: tokens feed components, components arrange into sections, sections assemble into full pages. Change something at the foundation, and everything built on top of it can follow, without needing to be rebuilt by hand. That's what makes a system maintainable long after launch, and what lets it scale alongside your team.

1. **Primitives**: the raw values. A hex code, a number, a font size. Nothing means anything yet.
2. **Semantic tokens**: a value with a job. A primitive becomes "surface-dark" or "text-on-light," a decision, not just a fact.
3. **Components**: built only from semantic tokens, never raw values. A button, a card, reusable anywhere.
4. **Sections**: components arranged for a purpose. A hero, a footer. Makes sense in one place, not everywhere.
5. **Templates**: sections assembled into a full page.

*Quick definitions (callout):* A component is one part, reusable anywhere. A section is parts arranged for a purpose, it only makes sense in one position. A template is sections stacked into a whole page. Rule of thumb: if it could move to another page unchanged, it's a component. If it only makes sense in one spot, it's a section.

**Section 2 — Why two token layers?**
Headline: *One says what it is. The other says what it's for.*
> A primitive is a value: #150040, 16px, a font weight. It doesn't know where it's used or why. A semantic token takes that value and gives it a job: "surface-dark," "text-on-light," "spacing-lg." That's context a primitive can never carry on its own.

> This distinction matters even more once AI enters the picture. An AI reading raw values has to guess at intent: is this hex code the brand's primary color, an error state, or a one-off? A semantic token removes the guesswork: it tells an agent exactly what a value is *for*, not just what it *is*. That's a large part of what "agent-ready" actually means in practice, not a separate technology, just a naming layer that turns facts into instructions an AI agent can follow correctly.

**Primitives — the raw values**
> The bottom of the cascade. A hex code, a pixel value, a font size: a fact, not a decision. Primitives don't carry meaning on their own; they're just what's available to build with.

**Semantic tokens — the decisions**
> A semantic token points to a primitive and gives it a job: "surface-dark," "text-on-light." This is where meaning enters the system, and where a brand decision lives separately from the raw value behind it.

**Section 3 — The agent contract**
Headline: *The knowledge layer that lets AI actually use your system*
> Most of what makes a design system usable never gets written down, it lives with your design team. Which pattern to reach for, when to bend a rule, what "on-brand" actually feels like is a judgment call. An AI can't access any of that unless it's captured somewhere it can read. This layer is the rules of the road: it tells an agent how to read your system and what to do with it, the same context that normally lives in your design team's heads, made available to machines.

> Three simple pieces:
- **The rules**: hard constraints on how tokens and components get used, so AI-generated work stays on-system by default, not by luck.
- **The guidelines**: the visual and UX judgment calls the rules can't cover. Where AI, and people, need context instead of just constraints.
- **The skill**: a short file that teaches AI when and how to use the system at all. Without it, even a perfectly built system is invisible to an AI trying to work with your brand.

*Callout:* Without this layer, a design system is just a resource sitting there, unread. With it, an agent can actually work inside your brand instead of guessing at it.

**Section 4 — What's inside it**
Headline: *What you're actually getting*
> Every system I build looks a little different, the right setup depends on your brand, your team, and what you're already working with. This is a general idea of what tends to be in place, not a fixed checklist.

> This is exactly why it isn't a click-of-a-button process. There's no one-size-fits-all template to drop in, what's actually needed shifts with the business, the team, and what already exists. The pieces below are a general shape, not a fixed order.

*Shown on the page as a diagram (not a table). Pieces:*

| Piece | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Token file | Every color, size, spacing, and type value, in one place | Change it once, it flows everywhere |
| Component library | The reusable pieces: buttons, cards, and so on, built from tokens | What your team and your AI tools actually build with |
| Design guidelines | The judgment calls: voice, layout, usage rules | Covers what pure rules can't |
| Agent contract | The rules and skill file that teach AI how to use the system safely | Keeps AI-generated work on-brand automatically |
| Living library | A browsable, always-current view of every token and component | If it's not here, it doesn't exist in the system yet |

**Section 5 — How it evolves**
Headline: *Not a one off quick fix, a new way to maintain your brand*
> A well-built system changes how your brand gets maintained going forward: update the foundation, and everything built on it updates too. Nobody has to hunt down every place it needs to change by hand.

> That easy upkeep isn't just there for when something breaks. It grows with you as your brand naturally evolves.

**Section 6 — What you can build**
Headline: *The real win: production without losing quality*
> This is the payoff everything else is building toward: your team using AI tooling to open up production, without losing what keeps the brand unique. Marketing can generate on-brand assets without waiting on design. Product can prototype without drifting off-pattern. Output scales without a bottleneck, and without a mess to clean up after.

> That's what skills make possible: small, focused instructions that teach an AI how to use your specific system for a specific job (a landing-page builder, a deck builder, a marketing-asset generator), each one inheriting the same tokens, components, and brand rules instead of reinventing them.

> This is also where the cost savings show up, concretely. An AI agent working from a connected system doesn't have to guess at your brand or have it explained in every request. It reads the structure once and gets it right. That means fewer tokens spent per request, higher first-pass accuracy, and less custom code getting generated and re-reviewed. Over dozens or hundreds of requests, that compounds: the foundation is what makes every use after it cheaper.

*Callout:* The better the foundation, the less every future request has to work to get it right.

*Diagram:* Design system → saved to your code repo (GitHub, or wherever your code lives) → referenced by separate skills: Deck builder, Landing page builder, One-pager builder.

## Case Study: Mixpanel (live at /mixpanel)

**Headline**
> Building an agentic design system for Mixpanel.

**Intro**
> I worked with Mixpanel to get its teams up and running with AI tooling. Leading the engagement end to end, I set the creative direction for the digital brand and built the system from the ground up: a full audit, design, an agentic design system, and the AI tooling the team now builds with.

**The problem — Where it started**
> Mixpanel's teams were ready to adopt AI, but output was slow and inconsistent. Without a shared source of truth, each team worked from its own repositories and tooling, often starting from scratch with every artifact, and legacy branding persisted across touchpoints.

> The project had two objectives: refine and elevate the brand across every surface while staying true to its foundations, and establish a system beneath it that is simple to maintain and dependable for both people and AI agents.

**Audit — Mapping what was really in use**
> I began by consolidating the full body of go-to-market output and reviewing it with stakeholders in a series of working sessions, assessing what was performing, what had fallen out of date, and which qualities to strengthen. These sessions established a shared direction and defined clear goals for the engagement.

**Design — One visual language for product and web**
> Working closely with core stakeholders at Mixpanel, I built a complete brand from the ground up, starting with a design system flexible enough to meet the needs of every team. I took a web-first approach, so teams could produce on-brand pages faster and with confidence. From that foundation, the same system now extends to a wider range of go-to-market assets, all while preserving the color and playfulness that define Mixpanel.

**Token system — Raw values, mapped to purpose**
> As the design direction took shape, I began structuring it into a coherent system. I captured the raw values from the design work as primitive tokens: the colors, type sizes and spacing that define the brand, each named for what it is. As the component library came together, I mapped those primitives to semantic tokens that give each value a clear purpose. The result is a cascading system: because the foundation links directly to the product, a single change at the root flows through to every surface, keeping maintenance simple. Every decision is intentional, and structured so AI agents can apply it correctly.

**Components & templates — Built on the system, not around it**
> This is where the system comes together. I built the component library in two forms: in Figma, for the team to design and iterate with, and in code, ready for AI agents to build from. Every component is bound to tokens, so agents can read the system clearly and apply it correctly. Templates then assemble these components into the patterns the team reuses most.

**Agent contract — The rules of the road, written for agents**
> Alongside the components, I built the material an AI agent needs to understand the system: a clear contract that sets out the rules of the road for both the brand and the design system, so agents know what to use, when, and why.

**Tooling & skills — A system AI tools can read**
> I built a suite of AI tooling and resources so the team could put the system to work from day one. The agentic design system acts as the source of truth, letting the team capture the productivity gains of AI while producing work that is on brand and ready to ship. To get them started, I built a set of skills, including a landing page builder with an integrated image library, so marketing and design can ship pages independently, consistently and on brand. I also created an ABM tool that tailors those pages to key prospect accounts, drawing on discovery call transcripts and pairing custom branding with Mixpanel's foundational brand to create a personalized experience for each prospect.


**The result**
> Mixpanel now has a strong foundation to build AI tooling on, and the team is putting the full power of AI production to work. Teams create high-quality, on-brand content independently, at a pace that simply wasn't possible before.

## Case Study: Coach App (live at /coach)

**Headline**
> Designing Coach, a mood and journal tracker powered by AI insights.

> I designed Coach end to end: the product, the design system behind it, and the way its AI insights are presented. The result is a mood and journal tracker that turns quick daily check-ins into patterns people can understand and act on.

- Company: Coach App · Year: 2026 · Services: Product design · UX research · Design system · AI insights

**The problem — Where it started**
> Most tracker and journal apps tell half the story. The data people log, like medication and daily habits, rarely connects to what they write about how they feel, so the patterns that matter stay hidden.

> My brief was to close that gap with a lightweight, focused product that surfaces only what's useful. Simple enough to become a daily ritual, and powerful enough to reveal insights and patterns that would otherwise go unnoticed.

**Research — Why people stop checking in**
> I started with a landscape audit: the best-in-class apps in the space, feature by feature, to map where the category already delivers and where it falls short. That audit set the scope for the product and gave me a clear foundation to plan from. From there, I built the first-stage user flow around only what the core experience needs.

**Design — Brand and product, designed together**
> I designed Coach around simplicity and ease of use. As an AI-native product, it uses AI to keep the conversation going, nudging people to go deeper when an area of their journaling needs attention. Alongside the journal, Coach tracks the factors that shape mood: medication consistency, or moderating substances like cigarettes for anyone working to quit. Mood and energy act as markers for what's working and what isn't. Every entry feeds the AI coach, which reads across all the data to surface patterns and insights that would otherwise be missed. The AI steps in where it adds value, while journaling and tracking stay at the centre of the experience.

**Token system — Raw values, mapped to purpose**
> Token building is where the design starts becoming code. Primitives hold the raw values: color, type, spacing and radius. Semantic tokens reference those primitives and name their purpose.

**Component building — Built on the tokens**
> From there, I built components, then patterns, then full pages: mood selectors, cards, inputs, buttons and navigation, each bound to tokens. Because every layer cascades from the one beneath it, the system stays easy to maintain: change a value once and it flows through the whole app. Components live in Figma for design and in code for engineering, so both stay in step.

**Stack and AI build — Built with AI, on the system**
> With the system in place, I moved into the build. AI tooling worked directly from the tokens, components and an agent contract, so generated screens matched the designs instead of drifting from them. The same setup lets the team keep shipping features that still feel like Coach, and extends to go-to-market resources built with AI that look and feel like the product. Everything stays on brand and consistent, so the team can focus on the work itself rather than the details of execution.

**AI tooling — The system, put to work**
> With a solid, reliable agentic design system in place, I could build and iterate on the product quickly while keeping every screen consistent. The team now has a foundation that lets them take full advantage of the speed of AI, with output that is production-ready and on brand from the start.

**The result — What changed**
> Coach now runs on a fully agent-ready design system that makes on-brand product development straightforward. New features and iterations come together quickly, unlocking the speed of AI development while preserving the human creative decisions that make the brand and product distinct.
